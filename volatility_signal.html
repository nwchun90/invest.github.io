Risk is not a penalty — it is a signal. High volatility means prices are more likely to overshoot, creating mispricings. But before you exploit that, you have to confirm the asset is actually swimming with the market's tide, not against it. This note builds the two tools — beta and sigma — that every later stage of this series leans on.
Two stocks each drop 10% in a week. For one, that's a red flag — sell before it gets worse. For the other, it's the best entry point you'll see all quarter. Same move, opposite meaning. Nothing in the price chart alone tells you which is which. You need two other numbers first — and this note is about what they are, and how to read them.
what is risk, really?

Most people hear "risk" and think "losing money." That's not wrong, but it's incomplete. In investing, risk really means uncertainty — how much a price tends to swing around its average, in either direction. A stock that barely moves has low risk. A stock that lurches up and down has high risk. That swinginess has a name: sigma (σ), or volatility.

Picture throwing darts at a board, once a day, where each dart lands wherever the stock's daily price change happens to be. A tight cluster of darts near the center means low volatility. Darts scattered all over the board mean high volatility.

σ = standard deviation of the asset's periodic returns The wider the curve, the further prices tend to stray from their average — in both directions.
Why this matters: risk cuts both ways mathematically — a stock can overshoot upward as easily as downward. Investors just feel the downside more.
The systematic view: a downside overshoot isn't automatically bad news. It can be the market temporarily mispricing something you already believe in.
The catch: that only holds if the asset is actually going up over time in the first place. Buying the dip on a stock in permanent decline just means owning more of a loser. That's what the next section checks.
beta (β) — are you swimming with the tide?

Before asking "how rough are the waves," ask a simpler question: is this asset even moving in the same direction as the market over time? That's what beta measures — whether a stock tends to rise and fall along with the broader market, or against it, and by how much.

β = 1.0 Moves exactly with the market Eligible
β > 1.0 Amplifies market moves — e.g. GOOGL ~1.05, TSLA ~1.8 Eligible
0 < β < 1.0 Dampens market moves — utilities, staples Eligible
β = 0 No relationship to the market at all Not eligible
β < 0 Moves opposite the market — a hedge Not eligible

A positive beta confirms the asset shares the market's long-term upward drift — it may be calmer or wilder than the market, but it's fundamentally rowing in the same direction. A beta at or below zero means the asset is a hedge, not a growth position, and the "buy the dip" logic from Section 1 no longer applies.

Formula A — the textbook definition
β = Cov(Rasset, Rmarket) / Var(Rmarket) How the asset's returns move together with the market's, scaled by how much the market itself moves.
Formula B — beta as risk, restated
β = ρasset,market × (σasset / σmarket) Correlation × the ratio of the two sigmas — this is where beta and Section 1's volatility connect directly.

How Formula B connects the dots

ρ (rho), correlation: a number between −1 and +1 that says how closely the asset's daily moves track the market's, regardless of size. It answers "same direction or not?"

σasset / σmarket: a ratio of the two sigmas from Section 1 — how wild the asset's swings are relative to the market's own swings. It answers "bigger or smaller moves?"

Multiply them together and you get beta: direction times relative size. A stock can have the exact same sigma as another and still have a completely different beta, if its correlation to the market differs.

Why it matters here: beta isn't a separate idea bolted onto volatility — it is volatility, filtered by direction. That's the mathematical link between "is this asset aligned with the market" and "how rough is this asset's ride," and it's why both numbers feed the same accumulation engine later in this series.

visualizing beta — the slope of the trendline

Each dot (not drawn) would be one day: market's return on the x-axis, the asset's return on the y-axis. Beta is simply the steepness — the slope — of the line that best fits those dots.

from beta to sigma — a different question

Knowing you're in the right boat doesn't tell you how rough the water is today. Beta compares an asset to the market. What we actually need next is something self-relative: how volatile is this asset compared to its own recent history? That's a different measurement, and it's the one that decides how aggressively to act right now.

σ_current
This month's temperature
A short, recent window of volatility — how jumpy has the price been lately?
σ_baseline
The normal range
A much longer average — what does "normal" volatility look like for this asset?

Illustrative pattern, not a specific ticker — shown to demonstrate the shape of the relationship, not to forecast any asset.

σ_current = StdDev of daily log returns over the last 60 periods, annualised (× √252)
σ_baseline = rolling average of σ_current over the last 252 periods A single volatility reading means nothing on its own — it only becomes useful once you compare it to what's normal for that same asset.
the risk throttle — turning volatility into a dial

Once you know today's volatility relative to normal, you can turn that comparison into a single dial that scales how aggressively a systematic strategy buys. That dial is called σ_t — the volatility ratio.

σ_t = clamp( σ_current ÷ σ_baseline , 0.5 , 2.5 ) Today's volatility divided by normal volatility, capped between half and two-and-a-half times.
0.5 — calm, scale down 1.0 — normal, no scaling 2.5 — panic, scale up 2.5×
σ_t = 1.0Volatility right at its average — neutral, no scaling
σ_t = 1.550% above normal — scale up 1.5×
σ_t = 2.5At the maximum clamp — scale up aggressively
σ_t = 0.5At the minimum clamp — scale down, stand back

The clamp exists so a single freak spike in volatility can't break the system — it caps how far the dial can swing in either direction.

buying fear, not chasing calm

The behavioural trap

What most people do: buy when things feel calm and prices are already high — then sell in a panic once prices fall and volatility spikes. That's buying tops and selling bottoms.

What a systematic approach does instead: deploy less capital when volatility is low and complacent, and more capital when volatility spikes — provided the asset still passes the beta check from Section 2.

Ahead of May, GOOGL's current volatility sat close to its own baseline — σ_t hovered near 1.0, meaning a systematic strategy would have been injecting at normal, un-scaled levels. As the stock pulled back roughly 16% from its all-time high into late June, volatility rose along with the drop, pushing σ_t above 1.0 — into the 1.4–1.6 range as an illustrative estimate of the direction of the move, not an exact computed reading.

Why the beta check still matters here: a rising σ_t on its own only says "this is moving more than usual." It says nothing about whether that move is a discount worth buying or the start of a structural collapse. GOOGL's positive beta (~1.05) is what tells you this pullback is more likely the former — an asset still fundamentally aligned with the market's long-term growth, currently on sale.
