the anthropic deal — stressed
⚠ Critical — Read This Carefully
$1.25B/month with Anthropic. Not locked in.
SpaceX's Colossus 1 data centre in Memphis has a deal with Anthropic — the AI company behind Claude — worth $1.25 billion per month, running until May 2029. That sounds like $45 billion in guaranteed revenue. It is not.
Material Uncertainty.
Elon Musk posted on X on May 28, 2026, directly contradicting the S-1's framing of the Anthropic deal. Musk wrote. "This is a 180 day lease with 90 day notice mutual cancellation thereafter. The short term was our request, not Anthropic's." Musk's statement directly contradicts SpaceX's S-1 filing, which presents the deal as running "through May 2029." The disclosure gap — the S-1 says three years, the CEO says six months???
The termination clause
Either party can exit with 90 days' notice — no penalty. There is no lock-in. The $45B total is a maximum possible value, not a guaranteed figure.
Anthropic is majority-funded by Amazon, which operates its own competing cloud (AWS). If Amazon steers Anthropic's compute to AWS, this contract evaporates.
Anthropic can also negotiate pricing down aggressively as more data centre supply comes online (Meta, Microsoft, Google all expanding). Renewal is not guaranteed.
Why it still matters
$1.25B/month is $15B/year if sustained — comparable to Starlink's 2025 revenue. Even 2 years of this contract ($30B) would materially change SpaceX's financial profile.
It proves there is real enterprise demand for SpaceX's AI compute infrastructure — not just a pitch deck. The contract exists today.
But at a $1.75T IPO valuation, the market appears to be pricing this as if it's guaranteed. The 90-day exit clause means it isn't.
the 5 thresholds — what has to go right
01
Power grid bottleneck
Colossus needs gigawatts of electricity. The Memphis power grid wasn't built for this. Expanding grid capacity takes 3–5 years. You can't rush the electricity authority. Production target of 1 million GPUs requires power supply that does not yet exist at that scale.
02
Funding limitation
Capex jumped from $5.6B (2024) to $20.7B (2025) — a near-fivefold increase. Total liabilities now exceed $60.5B. The IPO's $75B raise is partly needed to sustain this spending rate. If AI investment returns don't materialise fast enough, another capital raise is likely within 2 years.
03
Supply chain & engineering lead times
NVIDIA GPU allocations are rationed globally. Cooling systems, power electronics, and data centre components have 12–18 month lead times. SpaceX is not the only company competing for this supply — Meta, Microsoft, and Google are all ordering at scale simultaneously.
04
Talent — 9 of 11 xAI co-founders are gone
Since the merger completed in February 2026, nine of xAI's eleven original co-founders have departed. These were researchers from DeepMind, OpenAI, and Google Brain. The xAI CFO also left in April 2026. Rebuilding this team while integrating three companies simultaneously is a serious operational risk.
05
Orbital AI compute — the "big vision" isn't until 2028 at the earliest
SpaceX's long-term thesis involves putting AI data centres in space — satellites that process AI workloads in orbit using solar power. Timeline: demos late 2026, customer pilots 2027, volume deployment 2028 best case. Investors buying at IPO are funding a concept that won't generate revenue for at minimum 2 full years. This assumes Starship — which has never flown a commercial mission — also succeeds on schedule.

Banks have earned: (a) bridge loan interest, (b) underwriting fees. Their credit risk is transferred to public investors.
The conflict of interest in plain English: The five banks are simultaneously the lenders (who want the IPO to raise enough to repay their bridge loans) and the price-setters (who control what price the IPO is offered at). Their incentive is to price the IPO as high as possible — more proceeds repay more bridge debt and generate larger fees. There is no independent party representing what the stock is actually worth to a long-term holder. Public investors absorb the credit risk the banks assumed during the bridge phase. This is the "overdraft" of the valuation — the price is set to satisfy the financing chain, not to reflect fundamental value.

the verdict

Should you participate?

What is genuinely good: Starlink is a near-monopoly satellite internet business growing 50% annually with $7.2B EBITDA. The Anthropic compute contract proves real enterprise demand. The Colossus data centre, if the power and talent problems are solved, addresses the largest growing cost line in enterprise technology — AI compute.

What is genuinely concerning: At $1.75 trillion, you are paying 94× revenue for a company reporting a $4.94 billion annual loss. The underwriting banks have a structural incentive to overprice the IPO. The Anthropic contract is not locked in and can exit in 90 days. The orbital AI vision is 2+ years from generating a dollar of revenue. Nine of eleven xAI co-founders have departed. The power grid, GPU supply chain, and funding cycle all face hard constraints that cannot be solved by ambition alone.

The one-sentence verdict: Starlink alone is worth owning — but at $1.75 trillion, you are paying for three other businesses you may not want, at a price set by banks who need the IPO to succeed more than they need you to profit. If you participate, size small, expect volatility, and treat the lock-up expiry (180 days post-IPO) as your real entry point.
