four things to know before any of this makes sense
RULE 01
The tide line moves slow, on purpose
It's the average price over the last 200 days. One good day, or one bad week, barely nudges it. That's the point — it only cares about the big picture.
RULE 02
The fast boat overreacts to everything
It's an average of just the last 12 days, and it swings with every mood swing of the market — useful for noticing change early, useless for judging what's actually important. Its slightly steadier cousin is the "medium boat" you'll see next to it throughout.
RULE 03
A crossing isn't a signal — it's a symptom
When the fast boat crosses the medium boat, all that means is recent momentum shifted. It's a headline, not a verdict. What matters is which side of the tide line it happened on.
RULE 04
A pullback is a question, not an answer
Price dipping toward the medium boat or the tide line doesn't mean the tide turned. It might just mean the boat is catching its breath. The rest of this page is about telling those two apart.
the story — how price actually moves through this

The tide turns — price crosses the tide line from one side to the other. That alone tells you which direction the wind is generally blowing. The boat notices — the fast boat starts agreeing or disagreeing with the medium boat on that direction. Sometimes the boat overreacts — price sails way out from where the two boats are clustered, further and faster than makes sense, and every stretched-too-far move eventually needs to catch its breath. That's when the boat checks itself — price drifts back toward the medium boat or the tide line, and what happens next tells you almost everything: does it settle back onto its old course, does it just stumble and recover, or has the tide itself actually turned underneath it?

That four-part rhythm — tide turns, boat notices, boat overreacts, boat checks itself — is the entire idea, and it has a formal name: a state machine, moving through four states. Here's what it looks like mapped out:

the whole thing, one more time
Tide turnsPrice crosses the tide line. Sets the general direction, nothing more.
Boat noticesThe fast boat agrees or disagrees with the medium boat on that direction.
Boat overreactsPrice stretches unusually far from where the two boats are clustered.
Boat checks itselfPrice pulls back toward the medium boat or the tide line — and one of three things happens next:
— holds firmAbove the medium boat, same tide, story continues (Loop A).
— stumblesBelow the medium boat, above the tide line — not a reversal, just a wobble (shallow break).
— truly breaksBelow the tide line — the tide itself turns, everything resets (Loop B).
Worth remembering. Everything above has a real name. "The medium boat" and "the fast boat" — their crossover is the MACD line (Moving Average Convergence Divergence), one of the most widely used momentum indicators in investing. "The tide line" is the 200-day simple moving average, the classic long-term trend filter traders pair MACD with. Put together — MACD for momentum, SMA200 for the tide — and you have the exact framework this whole page just walked through in plain language.
"The tide doesn't ask what you think about it. It only asks which side you're standing on."
