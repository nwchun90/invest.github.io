if you bought tesla in january 2019

Imagine you had set aside $10,000 in January 2019 — and put every dollar into Tesla. No second-guessing, no stop-losses. Just buy and hold.

By May 2026, that $10,000 would have grown to roughly $223,600. That is a 22× return in seven years — a result most professional fund managers will never come close to matching in a career.

"A $10,000 bet on Tesla at the start of 2019 turned into more than $220,000 — without doing anything."

But here is the honest part: the ride was brutal. Tesla lost nearly half its value in the first three months of 2019 alone. It crashed again in early 2020 during COVID. Then, just when everyone felt comfortable after the 2021 rally, it fell more than 70% through 2022. Holding on through all of that required either extraordinary conviction — or a good strategy that made the discipline automatic.

not everyone can stomach one stock

Going 100% into any single company is not a strategy most people can actually live with — especially during a 73% drawdown. A more realistic approach is to split your money between two stocks that behave differently, and let a set of rules handle when to buy more and when to trim.

The question then becomes: which second stock? The choice matters enormously. A bad diversifier can drag your returns without giving you anything in return. A good one — one that moves differently to Tesla — can actually improve your returns, not just reduce your risk.

We tested two candidates: Broadcom (AVGO) and AngloGold Ashanti (AU). They were chosen for completely different reasons.

the real problem — timing the market

Why even a great business can destroy a good investor

Tesla clearing the bar as a value investment in 2019 was a necessary condition — not a sufficient one. A company worth owning long-term is only half the puzzle. The other half is entry: when you buy, how much you deploy, and what happens to your conviction the moment the stock falls 40%.

This is the core problem of market timing — and it is not a problem of intelligence. Even investors who correctly identify a great business routinely destroy their own returns by buying too much at peaks, selling too early during drawdowns, or simply running out of patience before the thesis plays out.

The lump-sum Tesla investor in January 2019 was down 25% by March. They were down again in March 2020. And again — massively — through all of 2022. At each of those moments, the correct move was to buy more. Very few did. The information was available. The nerve was not.

This is exactly what VAA with internal rebalancing solves. A company that qualifies under value investing principles — durable moat, long-term growth, reasonable entry — already satisfies the "what." VAA removes the "when" from the equation entirely. The rules force you to buy more when it hurts most — automatically, without discretion.

The honest framing: VAA does not replace stock selection — it amplifies it. A mediocre business run through VAA will still underperform. The strategy only works because Tesla was genuinely worth owning over a 7-year horizon. What VAA adds is mechanical robustness — an algorithm that never panics, never overtrades, and never misses the months that matter most. The investor's job is to pick the right business. The strategy's job is to eliminate the timing risk of owning it.

choosing the right partner
BROADCOM (AVGO)  ·  THE NEAR-EQUAL
A rival performer — almost as strong as Tesla
AVGO returned 12.4× over the same period when you factor in its generous dividends — not far behind Tesla's 22.4×. Broadcom builds the chips that power data centres and AI workloads, giving it a completely different business engine to Tesla's cars and energy products. A strong second stock alongside Tesla means you are not giving up much return in exchange for the discipline benefits of holding two assets.
ANGLOGOLD ASHANTI (AU)  ·  THE SAFE HARBOUR
A gold miner that zigs when Tesla zags
AU returned 6.8× — far behind Tesla — but that is not why it is here. AngloGold is a gold mining company, and gold tends to rise when investors panic and sell growth stocks. In the COVID crash of 2020 and the rate-hike selloff of 2022, AU moved in the opposite direction to Tesla. This opposite movement is the key ingredient in a strategy that benefits from volatility — and AU has plenty of it.

What makes a good second stock — the formula behind it

Higher return asset gets more weight. Our formula automatically gives more of your money to whichever of the two stocks has performed better recently. The underperformer gets a smaller slice — enough to do its job as a ballast, but not so much it drags the whole portfolio.

More volatile stocks earn a bigger role. Counterintuitively, we want the diversifier to swing a lot. Every time it swings far from Tesla, it creates a chance to rebalance — sell the winner, buy the loser at a discount. The bigger the swings, the bigger that harvesting opportunity.

Correlation is the key. When two stocks move together, there is nothing to harvest. AU and TSLA frequently move in opposite directions — that gap is where the extra return comes from. AVGO moves more similarly to Tesla, but its raw strength as a performer makes it useful in a different way.

how the strategy works — in plain english

Value Averaging Accumulation (VAA): Instead of investing a fixed amount every month regardless of price, VAA sets a target for what your portfolio should be worth each month. The target grows by $500 per month. If your portfolio falls short of the target — because the stock dropped — you invest the exact difference to catch up. If the portfolio is already above target, you do nothing and keep your cash. This means you automatically buy more when prices are low and hold back when prices are high.

Internal Rebalancing: When one stock in the pair drifts more than 10% away from its target weight, the strategy sells a little of the winner and uses that money to buy more of the laggard — entirely within the portfolio, with no extra cash from you. There is one guard: if the winner is above its 12-month average price (a sign it is still in a strong uptrend), the trim is skipped for that month so you do not cut a winner short.

The combined effect: VAA buys more Tesla when it crashes. Rebalancing automatically shifts money into whichever of the two stocks is lagging. Together, they create a mechanical discipline that does what most investors find emotionally impossible — buying aggressively during the bad months.

what these numbers are telling us
The VAA + Broadcom combination was the outright winner. It turned $7,186 of actual cash into $252,300 — a 35× return, better than buying Tesla alone. The key was AVGO's strong independent performance: when Tesla stumbled, AVGO often held up, and the rebalancing shifted money from AVGO back into Tesla at lower prices. That cycle, repeated month after month, generated real extra return.
The VAA + AngloGold combination is a very different story. It kept pace with the Tesla lump sum (23× vs 22.4×) while deploying $2,800 less cash. The gold miner protected the portfolio during the worst crashes — but its lower long-term return meant the rebalancing premium was partially offset by persistently shifting money toward the weaker performer.
Pure lump-sum Tesla was still exceptional — just not the most efficient. It required all $10,000 upfront and offered no mechanism to take advantage of the 2022 crash. Someone who simply held never had the chance to buy Tesla at $123 (its December 2022 low).
Broadcom alone beat AngloGold alone handily (12.4× vs 6.8×). But as a partner to Tesla, AngloGold brings something AVGO cannot: reliable opposite movement during crises. Choosing between them depends entirely on what you want the second asset to do — contribute return, or provide ballast.
bottom line

A simple lump-sum in Tesla produced extraordinary results — but it demanded iron nerves through a 73% drawdown and left no room to buy more during the crash. The systematic VAA approach, pairing Tesla with Broadcom and letting a fixed set of rules decide when to invest and rebalance, delivered a higher return on the cash actually used — and did it by following a repeatable process rather than requiring any market timing. The gold miner pairing, while less profitable in this specific period, offered genuine crash protection and still matched the lump-sum Tesla result, with $2,800 left undeployed.

One caveat: these results rely on estimated historical prices and the benefit of hindsight. The strategy rules are mechanical and would have required real discipline — particularly during the months when Tesla was down 50% and every instinct said to stop investing. The numbers work because the rules forced buying at exactly those moments.
